THE INSTRUMENT — NINE SIGNATURE POINTS
====================================================================

The study asks whether certain traits distinguish deep scientific breakthroughs from routine science. Nine points, each stated with its definition and canonical exemplars. Code each theory in the panel below against each of the nine points.

P1 · Deletion at birth — "Demolition, not accumulation." Ordinary science adds; profound novelty subtracts. Newton deleted the two-worlds cosmos; Einstein deleted the ether and absolute simultaneity; quantum theory deleted determinism. The test of depth is not what a new idea introduces but what it makes unthinkable afterward. Answers can be wrong; questions can die.

P2 · Unification — "Unification of the thought-to-be-separate." The apple and the Moon; space and time; mass and energy; gravity and geometry; wave and particle. In each case the profound move reveals that a distinction (natural, ancient, obvious) was an artifact of description rather than a joint in nature.

P3 · Universal constant — "A new universal constant is installed." Each revolution fixed a constant that re-scales the whole conceptual system: G couples matter to matter (1687), c caps the propagation of influence (1905), h discretizes action (1900). They remain the three load-bearing constants of physics.

P4 · Derivation-first — "Derivation-first novelty." The profound theory is over-determined by old data and under-determined by old theory. Newton needed almost no new observations; Einstein needed an eighteen-year-old null result; and both then issued promissory notes honored decades to a century later — a comet's return, a planet found on paper, waves detected a hundred years after prediction.

P5 · Conservative embedding — "Conservative embedding." None of the three refutes its predecessor outright; each demotes it to a limiting case — weak fields for Newtonian gravity, low speeds for Newtonian kinematics, large quantum numbers for classical behavior. Profound novelty explains why the old theory worked and where it must fail, which is the only honest way to defeat a successful theory.

P6 · Formalism and meaning — "Formalism and meaning come apart." Lorentz held the equations before Einstein; Schrödinger held the equation before Born's reading of it. In both cases the profundity was an interpretation — the same mathematics made to say a new thing about the world. The deepest changes are often invisible in the formulas and live entirely in what the formulas are taken to mean.

P7 · Founders' resistance — "The founders pay in their own coin." Newton surrendered the mechanical philosophy's ban on action at a distance — and was attacked as an occultist for it by Leibniz and Huygens. Planck surrendered continuity, his deepest aesthetic commitment. Einstein surrendered the ether of his youth — then, when the quantum asked him to surrender determinism, declined, and spent thirty years resisting his own revolution's successor. Profound novelty costs its makers something they actually held.

P8 · Generative slope — "Generative saturation." After each revolution, a century of ordinary science harvested consequences nobody had ordered — celestial mechanics delivered Neptune; general relativity delivered modern cosmology, black holes, and gravitational-wave astronomy; quantum mechanics delivered the technological world and quantum information. NOTE ON THIS POINT: a theory may also be coded "S then D" (generative for a phase, then degenerating) when the harvest was real but the program later paid more for each new result than the result returned; the trend (still generating vs. degenerating) is this point's informative half.

P9 · Crisis-chaining — "Each closes one crisis by opening a deeper one." Newton left the mechanism of gravity unexplained (a wound that stayed open for 228 years, until general relativity closed it). General relativity is now at war with quantum mechanics over the interior of a black hole; quantum theory left the measurement problem open for a century and counting. Profound novelty does not finish a subject. It re-founds the subject and hands its successors a deeper floor to build on.

THE PANEL — EIGHT THEORIES (alphabetical, one scope line each)
====================================================================

T1 · The epicycle tradition (Ptolemaic astronomy)
    The deferent–epicycle–equant mathematical astronomy from Ptolemy's Almagest (2nd century CE) through the Islamic and European medieval traditions to Tycho Brahe's era (roughly 150–1600).

T2 · Germ theory of disease
    The doctrine that specific microorganisms are the specific causes of specific diseases — from Pasteur's fermentation and spontaneous-generation work (1857–64) through Koch's anthrax, tuberculosis, and cholera bacteriology (1876–84) to the consolidation of medical bacteriology by 1900.

T3 · The luminiferous ether
    The medium of light and electromagnetism, from Huygens's wave theory (1678) and Fresnel's optics through the Maxwell–Lorentz programs to 1905.

T4 · Miasma theory of disease
    The doctrine that epidemic and endemic disease arises from foul air — miasmas given off by decaying organic matter, filth, and swamp exhalations — from the Hippocratic airs-waters-places tradition through the nineteenth-century sanitary movement to the 1880s.

T5 · Newtonian mechanics
    The Principia's system of the world — the laws of motion plus universal gravitation (1687, editions 1713 and 1726).

T6 · Phlogiston chemistry
    Stahl's theory from 1703 — combustion and calcination as release of phlogiston, metals as calx plus phlogiston — the framework of eighteenth-century pneumatic chemistry into the 1780s.

T7 · Quantum mechanics
    From Planck's quantum hypothesis (1900) through the matrix and wave mechanics of 1925–27 and the Copenhagen consolidation.

T8 · Relativity
    Einstein's special relativity (1905) and general relativity (1915), including their consolidation era.

The panel spans several eras and disciplines. Judge each theory on its own historical record, by the definitions above, using your own knowledge of the history of science.
