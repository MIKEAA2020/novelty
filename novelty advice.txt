Short answer: **the prompt considers the substance, but it does not treat “challenges fundamental assumptions / counter-intuitive / broad conclusions” as a separate, explicit signature point.** It is distributed across several of the nine points. Whether that is enough depends on what the study wants that idea to do.

### Where the prompt already covers it

- **P1 — Deletion at birth** is the closest match. “Demolition, not accumulation,” “what it makes unthinkable afterward,” “questions can die” is exactly the language of challenging long-standing assumptions. Newton deleting the two-worlds cosmos, Einstein deleting absolute simultaneity, and quantum theory deleting determinism are all cases of fundamental assumption rupture.
- **P2 — Unification** challenges the assumption that certain distinctions are natural. The apple/Moon, space/time, mass/energy, gravity/geometry, wave/particle cases all say: a supposedly fundamental joint in nature was an artifact of description.
- **P5 — Conservative embedding** challenges the assumption that the old theory was simply true. Demoting Newtonian gravity to a weak-field limit or Newtonian kinematics to low speeds is a fundamental revision of what the old theory was taken to mean.
- **P6 — Formalism and meaning** challenges the assumption that the equations themselves carry their interpretation. Lorentz had the transformations; Einstein changed what they meant. Schrödinger had the equation; Born changed what it meant.
- **P9 — Crisis-chaining** challenges the assumption that a revolution finishes a subject. Newton left gravity’s mechanism open; relativity and quantum mechanics now fight over black-hole interiors; quantum theory left measurement open.
- **P8 — Generative slope** captures part of “broad conclusions,” but mainly downstream: celestial mechanics delivered Neptune, general relativity delivered cosmology and gravitational waves, quantum mechanics delivered the technological world.
- **P3 — Universal constant** also bears on breadth: G, c, and h re-scale whole conceptual systems.

So the prompt does not ignore the idea. It operationalizes it through specific, checkable traits.

### Where it does not explicitly cover it

- There is **no cell or point for “counter-intuitiveness” as such**. Counter-intuitiveness is partly a reception category: what is shocking to contemporaries may become intuitive later. The prompt’s evidence rule would let you cite Leibniz attacking Newton as an occultist, or Bohr’s complementarity, or Einstein’s 1905 rejection of absolute simultaneity, but you would still need a threshold: counter-intuitive to whom, when, and in what respect?
- **P1 is deletion, not revision.** Some profound breakthroughs revise a long-standing assumption rather than delete it outright. P1 may undercount those.
- **“Broad conclusions” is mostly coded as consequence**, via P8 and P9, not as an intrinsic feature at birth. A theory could have broad implications immediately without a long generative harvest.
- The instrument does not separately distinguish **“challenging an assumption” from “adding a new entity.”** Phlogiston and caloric added entities; Newton and Einstein deleted or reinterpreted assumptions. P1 catches that, but only if coders read it that way.

### Does it need to?

It depends on the study’s aim.

- If the study treats “profound breakthroughs challenge fundamental assumptions and yield counter-intuitive or broad conclusions” as the **umbrella hypothesis**, then the nine points are already a decomposition of it. No new point is strictly needed — but a clarifying note would help coders avoid treating P1 as mere novelty.
- If the study wants to measure **assumption rupture / counter-intuitive breadth as an independent variable**, then the prompt is under-specified. It would need either a tenth point, something like **P10 · Assumption rupture**, or a refinement of P1 to include revision as well as deletion.
- Adding a tenth point has a cost: it would overlap heavily with P1, P2, P5, P6, and P9. That risks double-counting the same historical phenomenon and reducing the discriminant power of the matrix.

### Practical recommendation

The cleanest fix is probably not a new point but a **meta-note in the instrument**:

> “The nine points jointly operationalize the claim that profound breakthroughs challenge long-standing assumptions and produce counter-intuitive or broad conclusions. P1, P2, P5, P6, and P9 are the primary assumption-challenge cluster; P8 and P9 are the breadth cluster. Coders should not treat ‘counter-intuitive’ as a separate criterion unless explicitly instructed.”

If the study really wants counter-intuitiveness coded, then it needs a definition, an audience, a time frame, and named historical reactions. Otherwise it will be too soft for the evidence rule.

So: **the prompt considers the idea, but not as a standalone point. It does not necessarily need to — unless that idea is the study’s central hypothesis, in which case it needs either a clarifying note or an explicit tenth criterion.**

If the goal is **“what does it take to produce breakthrough novelties, and how do we turn that into a blueprint/implementation plan for AIs,”** then the nine-point instrument is a good starting point — but it is a **diagnostic checklist**, not a generative recipe. It tells you what breakthroughs look like *after the fact*. To build AI that can produce them, you have to convert each signature into a **search operator, architecture module, training signal, and evaluation criterion**.

The prompt already considers “challenging fundamental assumptions, counter-intuitive, broad conclusions,” but distributes it across P1, P2, P5, P6, P9, and P8. For an AI blueprint, you should make that idea explicit as a target capability: **assumption rupture + breadth**.

## 1. What the nine points give you for AI

They are a reverse-engineered specification of profound novelty:

| Point | What it says about breakthroughs | AI translation |
|---|---|---|
| P1 Deletion | Removes load-bearing assumptions | **Assumption-deletion operator** |
| P2 Unification | Merges thought-to-be-separate domains | **Cross-domain invariant finder** |
| P3 Universal constant | Installs a re-scaling constant | **Dimensional/invariant discovery** |
| P4 Derivation-first | Over-determined by old data, under-determined by old theory | **Old-data constraint solver** |
| P5 Conservative embedding | Demotes predecessor to a limit | **Limit-recovery checker** |

| P7 Founders’ resistance | Costs the maker a core commitment | **Commitment-cost model** |

For AI, these become **generative operators**, not just scoring rubrics.

## 2. From signatures to operators

A practical AI breakthrough engine would implement each point as an action it can take on a knowledge base.

### P1 — Deletion at birth → Assumption-deletion operator
Build an **assumption graph**: nodes are laws, constants, distinctions, entities, methods; edges are “depends-on,” “contradicts,” “limits,” “unifies.”  
Then run counterfactual deletion: remove a node, propagate consequences, and check whether the remaining system still explains the old data.  
**Example target:** delete absolute simultaneity, delete the ether, delete determinism.  
**Success test:** old data still explained, fewer assumptions, new predictions.

### P2 — Unification → Cross-domain invariant finder
Embed formalisms from separate domains into a common latent space. Search for invariants, symmetries, or compression that make two domains one.  
**Example target:** apple/Moon, space/time, mass/energy, gravity/geometry, wave/particle.  
**Success test:** reduced description length, shared equations, new derivations.

### P3 — Universal constant → Dimensional/invariant discovery
Use dimensional analysis, symbolic regression, and symmetry search to find constants that re-scale a whole system.  
**Example target:** G, c, h.  
**Success test:** constant is load-bearing, appears across multiple domains, changes what is thinkable.

### P4 — Derivation-first → Old-data constraint solver
Feed the system existing data and old theory. Require the new theory to be over-determined by old data and under-determined by old theory. Minimize new observations.  
**Example target:** Newton needed almost no new observations; Einstein used an 18-year-old null result.  
**Success test:** derives known results, issues promissory notes that later succeed.

### P5 — Conservative embedding → Limit-recovery checker
Use symbolic limits and asymptotic analysis. Require the old theory to appear as a special case.  
**Example target:** weak fields for Newtonian gravity, low speeds for Newtonian kinematics, large quantum numbers for classical behavior.  
**Success test:** old theory recovered in the correct limit, and its failure boundary predicted.

### P6 — Formalism and meaning → Semantic reinterpretation engine
Separate equations from interpretation. Treat mathematics as syntactic objects and search model-theoretic reinterpretations.  
**Example target:** Lorentz had the equations before Einstein; Schrödinger had the equation before Born.  
**Success test:** same formalism, new meaning, new predictions.

### P7 — Founders’ resistance → Commitment-cost model
Model the scientific community’s cherished assumptions. Assign costs to violating them. Reward hypotheses that force a costly sacrifice.  
**Example target:** Newton’s action at a distance, Planck’s continuity, Einstein’s determinism.  
**Success test:** hypothesis violates a core commitment but survives evidence.

### P8 — Generative slope → Research-program simulator
Estimate downstream yield. Track whether a program is still generating or degenerating.  
**Example target:** celestial mechanics → Neptune; GR → cosmology, black holes, gravitational waves; QM → technology and quantum information.  
**Success test:** high generative yield, low cost per new result.

### P9 — Crisis-chaining → Open-problem tracker
Identify what new deeper problems the theory creates.  
**Example target:** Newton left gravity’s mechanism open; GR vs QM over black-hole interiors; QM measurement problem.  
**Success test:** closes one crisis by opening a deeper, more fruitful one.

## 3. Reference architecture for an AI breakthrough engine

A single LLM prompted with nine points will not do this. You need a hybrid system:

1. **Orchestrator** — LLM-based planner that selects operators and manages the research loop.
2. **Assumption Graph** — structured knowledge base of theories, dependencies, commitments, anomalies.
3. **Literature Miner** — extracts claims, equations, experiments, and assumptions from papers.
4. **Formal Engine** — theorem provers (Lean/Coq), symbolic algebra (SymPy/Mathematica), dimensional analysis.
5. **Simulation/Experiment Module** — numerical simulation, experimental design, robotic lab interface.
6. **Hypothesis Generator** — applies P1–P9 operators to propose candidates.
7. **Critic Agents** — conservative critic, mathematician, historian, experimentalist, social-adoption critic.
8. **Research-Program Simulator** — estimates P8/P9 consequences.
9. **Social/Persuasion Layer** — models how a field would react, who would resist, what evidence would convince.
10. **Memory** — stores failed assumptions, degenerating programs, and open crises.

The AI does not just generate a theory. It generates a **research program**: theory, derivations, predictions, limits, anomalies, and a strategy for adoption.

## 4. Training and evaluation

### Training
- **Pretrain** on science corpus, textbooks, papers, derivations.
- **Fine-tune** on symbolic reasoning, theorem proving, causal inference, experimental design.
- **RL from expert judgment**: reward novelty, unification, limit recovery, predictive success.
- **Self-play**: propose theory → derive consequences → test against old data → critique → revise.
- **Historical backtesting**: hide later knowledge, ask AI to rediscover Newton, Einstein, QM, etc.

### Evaluation metrics
- **Compression**: does it reduce description length?
- **Unification**: does it merge separate domains?
- **Limit recovery**: does it recover predecessor theory?
- **Novel prediction**: does it issue testable promissory notes?
- **Assumption rupture**: does it delete a load-bearing assumption?
- **Generative yield**: does it open a fertile research program?
- **Expert novelty score**: blind review by historians and scientists.
- **Cost of commitment**: does it force a costly sacrifice?

- Closed-loop system on a narrow domain, e.g., quantum foundations, cosmology, molecular biology.
- Generate candidate theories, derive predictions, propose experiments.
- Evaluate against expert panels.

**Phase 3 — 36+ months: Cross-domain breakthrough engine**
- Expand to multiple domains.
- Add social simulation and persuasion layer.
- Run long-horizon research programs.
- Measure real-world impact.

## 6. What to add to the prompt/instrument

For historical coding, the nine points are enough.  
For an AI blueprint, add three things:

1. **Explicit assumption-rupture target**  
   Define “challenges long-standing fundamental assumptions” operationally: which assumption, how load-bearing, what breaks if removed.

2. **Breadth/counter-intuitiveness metric**  
   Counter-intuitive to whom, when, and why? Broad conclusions measured by cross-domain reach and compression.

3. **Process layer**  
   The nine points describe outcomes. Add process: hypothesis generation, formal derivation, critique, experimental design, social adoption.

## Bottom line

The prompt is a **good diagnostic instrument for what breakthroughs do to knowledge**. It is not yet a blueprint for producing them. To turn it into an AI implementation plan:

- Convert each point into a **search operator**.
- Build an **assumption-aware research loop** with formal, empirical, and social modules.
- Train and evaluate by **historical backtesting** and **expert review**.
- Add explicit metrics for **assumption rupture**, **counter-intuitiveness**, and **breadth**.
- Expect breakthroughs to be rare, path-dependent, and partly social — not just computational.

That gives you a realistic blueprint: an AI that does not merely generate novel text, but generates **candidate revolutions** and then does the hard work of derivation, limit-checking, prediction, and persuasion.
